EMERGENCY TIPS

  1. Build a 72-hour emergency kit

    Include water, non-perishable food, flashlight, batteries, and first-aid supplies. Rescue and relief teams often can't reach everyone right away, so you need to survive independently for the first few days.

  2. Learn basic first aid and CPR

    Bleeding control, burn treatment, and CPR skills can keep someone alive before help arrives. Many emergency deaths happen in the critical window before professional responders show up.

  3. Map out two evacuation routes

    Identify a primary and backup way out of your home, school, or workplace. Fires, debris, or flooding can block one path, so having an alternative avoids getting trapped.

  4. Set up a family communication plan

    Choose a meeting point and an out-of-town contact everyone can reach. Local networks get overloaded in disasters, but distant contacts are often easier to reach and can relay messages.

  5. Know your local hazards

    Research whether your area is prone to earthquakes, floods, typhoons, or fires. This lets you prepare specific responses instead of generic ones that may not fit your actual risk.

  6. Practice emergency drills

    Rehearse fire escapes, earthquake "duck, cover, hold," or lockdown procedures regularly. Repetition builds muscle memory, which takes over when stress and adrenaline impair clear thinking.

  7. Back up important documents

    Keep copies of IDs, insurance, and medical records in a waterproof folder or cloud storage. These are difficult to replace and often required for aid or insurance claims after a disaster.

  8. Learn utility shut-off procedures

    Know how to turn off gas, water, and electricity at the source. A damaged line left on can cause fires, explosions, or flooding after events l ike earthquakes.

  9. Keep your vehicle prepared

    Maintain a half-full tank and pack a car kit (blanket, water, jumper cables). Fuel shortages and gridlock are common during evacuations, and being stranded unprepared adds real danger.

  10. Follow trusted information sources

    Rely on official agencies, weather services, or verified alert apps rather than social media rumors. Bad information during a crisis can lead to worse decisions than having no information at all.

EMERGENCY REMINDER

  1. Stay calm and think before acting

    Panic leads to poor decisions like running into danger or freezing up. Taking a breath first helps you assess the situation clearly.

  2. Don't use elevators during fires or earthquakes

    Elevators can lose power or become trapped between floors. Stairwells are safer even though they're slower.

  3. Check yourself and others for injuries first

    Before moving or helping someone else, ensure you're not injured yourself. You can't help others effectively if you become a second casualty.

  4. Keep your phone charged and carry a power bank

    Communication and access to alerts depend on battery life. A dead phone during a crisis cuts you off from help and information.

  5. Don't return to a danger zone for belongings

    Possessions can be replaced; lives cannot. Many injuries occur when people go back inside burning or unstable structures for items.

  6. Turn off gas and electricity if you smell gas or see sparks

    Delaying this increases fire or explosion risk. Acting immediately, if it's safe to do so, prevents a small problem from escalating.

  7. Move to higher ground during flood warnings

    Floodwaters can rise faster than expected, and even shallow fast-moving water can sweep people away. Don't wait to see how bad it gets.

  8. Avoid downed power lines

    Treat all fallen lines as live and stay well away. Electrocution risk remains even if the line looks inactive.

  9. Keep emergency contacts written down, not just saved digitally

    Phones can be lost, damaged, or run out of battery. A physical copy ensures you still have access to critical numbers.

  10. Listen for official "all clear" signals before returning

    Aftershocks, gas leaks, or structural damage may not be immediately visible. Returning too early can put you back in danger.
